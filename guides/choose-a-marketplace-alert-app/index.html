Choosing an app

How to choose a Facebook Marketplace alert app

What to check before you pay for a Marketplace alert app: how often it checks, radius or per-town searches, what an alert shows, filters, trial and cost.

By the Zoopy team Updated 26 September 2026 9 min read

Quick answer

Judge a Marketplace alert app on how it works, not on its speed claims. Check how often it checks and whether that is a target, whether it admits Facebook’s public-search delay, whether one search covers an area and radius, what an alert shows, which filters read the description, and that it never asks for your Facebook login.

How do Marketplace alert apps work?

Facebook gives you one free option: save a search, turn on Marketplace notifications, and wait for Facebook to tell you about new listings. Our guide to Facebook Marketplace alerts walks through it, and this one covers what to do when those notifications go quiet.

A dedicated alert app does the searching for you. It runs your search on its own schedule against public Marketplace listings, spots the ones it has not seen before, checks them against your filters and sends you a push notification. Every app does roughly that. The differences that matter are how often it looks, how much ground one search covers, how much of the listing it reads, what it sends you, and what it asks of you in return.

1. How often does it check, and is that a promise or a target?

“Every 3 minutes” can mean a check starts every three minutes without fail, or that the app aims for that when its servers are not busy. Ask which. Then ask what happens to a wide search: is every part of the area checked at that pace, or only the middle? If faster checking costs more, ask how many of your searches get it.

2. Is it honest about Facebook’s public-search delay?

In our testing, Facebook keeps a brand-new listing out of public search for about six to seven minutes after the seller posts it. That wait applies to everyone, including every alert app and anyone refreshing by hand. An app that checks very often still cannot show you a listing Facebook has not released yet.

So ask one question: how long from the moment the seller posts to the moment the alert reaches you, and where and when was that measured? Speed quoted from when the app found the listing, rather than when the seller posted it, hides the part you care about.

3. One search per town, or one area and a radius?

Marketplace searches one place and radius at a time. Some apps copy that, so covering a region can take a separate search for each town. Others take one point and a radius. Ask how the app counts a “search”, and how many you need for your real hunt, for example every Toyota HiLux within 250 km of Toowoomba. Our wide-area search guide shows how to plan the coverage.

4. What does an alert show?

A useful alert lets you decide from the lock screen: a photo, the price, the key details (kilometres and gearbox for a car) and how far away it is. Ask to see a sample. An alert that is just a link makes you open every one to find out it is a dud.

5. Does it read the whole listing before it alerts you?

A search results page shows little more than the photo, title and price. The words that change your mind, like “needs work”, “no rego” or “for parts”, usually sit in the description. An app that matches on the title alone cannot filter on them.

6. Which filters does it have?

At minimum you want a price range, words to exclude, and for cars make, model, year and kilometres. Better apps can also hide dealer, salvage and damaged listings, and support rules such as “HiLux AND (SR5 OR Rogue) NOT wrecking”. Ask whether a filtered listing disappears or waits in a feed you can check later. Our guides on filtering out dealers and spotting written-off cars explain why these two filters matter.

7. Does it need your Facebook login?

It should not. Marketplace listings are public, so an alert app only needs to read them. Giving an app your password, or a login code sent to your phone, gives it your account, and with it the power to post and message as you. Facebook’s own scam guide lists requests for verification codes and account passwords as phishing. When you want to contact a seller, you should do it yourself, in Facebook.

8. Does it run on your phone, in a browser, or on its own servers?

This decides whether it works while you sleep. A browser extension only checks while your computer is on and the browser is open. An app that checks from your phone depends on the phone letting it run in the background, and on iPhone, Low Power Mode turns Background App Refresh off. An app that runs on its own servers keeps checking whatever your phone is doing; your phone only receives the push.

9. What does it cost, and what is in the trial?

Find the price per month, in which currency, and what counts toward it: searches, areas or speed. For the trial, check its length, whether it needs a card, and whether trial users get the same speed as paying users. On the App Store, Apple says to cancel at least 24 hours before a trial ends if you do not want it to renew.

10. How do you cancel?

On iPhone, subscriptions bought through the App Store are cancelled in Settings, under your name, then Subscriptions. On Android, open Subscriptions in Google Play. Google notes that uninstalling an app does not cancel its subscription. If an app bills your card directly on a website, find the cancel button before you pay, not after.

11. Does it handle your currency and units?

If you buy outside the app’s home country, check that listing prices show in your currency, that distances come in kilometres or miles as you prefer, and that the subscription price shown is the one your store will charge.

12. What does it do with your data?

Every App Store page has an App Privacy section and every Google Play page has a Data safety section. Both are written by the developer, so read the privacy policy too: what it collects, whether it sells anything, where the data is stored, and whether you can delete your account inside the app.

Red flags that should make you pause

  • Speed claims with no date, place or starting point.
  • Promises that you will not miss anything. Nobody sees Marketplace completely.
  • A “best apps” list on the app’s own website with itself at number one.
  • Star ratings shown on its own site from a handful of reviews.
  • Any request for your Facebook password or a code sent to your phone.
  • No named company, no contact address and no privacy policy.

Test two apps on the same search

If two apps offer trials, run them side by side on the same search for a few days. Note which alert arrives first for the same listing and which one catches listings the other misses. Set a reminder to cancel the one you drop before its trial ends.

Questions people ask

Is there a free Facebook Marketplace alert app?

Facebook's own saved searches and Marketplace notifications are free, and our alerts guide shows how to set them up. Dedicated alert apps usually charge a subscription because checking searches around the clock costs money to run, and many offer a free trial.

Do Marketplace alert apps need my Facebook login?

They should not. Marketplace listings are public, so an alert app only needs to read them. Never give any app your Facebook password or a login code.

How fast can a Marketplace alert app be?

No faster than Facebook shows the listing. In our testing, Facebook keeps a new listing out of public search for about six to seven minutes after it is posted, so an honest app measures its speed from the seller's posting time and includes that wait.

Should an alert app run on my phone or on its own servers?

An app that checks from its own servers keeps going while your phone is locked, in Low Power Mode or switched off. An app or browser extension that does the checking on your device only works while that device lets it run.

How do I cancel a Marketplace alert app subscription?

If you subscribed through the App Store, open Settings, tap your name, then Subscriptions. On Android, open Subscriptions in Google Play. Deleting or uninstalling the app does not cancel it.
