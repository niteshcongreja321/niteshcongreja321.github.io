Deals and flipping

Flipping on Facebook Marketplace

How flipping on Facebook Marketplace works: what sells, how to price a buy, the costs to count, safe pickups, and how to watch for stock without scrolling.

By the Zoopy team Updated 26 September 2026 11 min read

Quick answer

Flipping means buying something on Marketplace for less than it usually sells for, then reselling it after every cost. It works best with items you know, that are easy to price and quick to test. Set your top buy price from real sale prices minus fuel, time, repairs, fees and tax, and meet safely.

How does flipping on Facebook Marketplace work?

You buy an item from a private seller, clean it, fix it if needed, photograph it well and sell it again. What you keep is the sale price minus everything the item cost you, including your time.

That last part is the one people skip. Flipping is a small trade with real costs, not free money, and this guide makes no promise that it will pay.

Prices in this guide are in Australian dollars and are illustrative. Use your own local prices.

What sells well for flippers?

The best flips share four traits: a clear model name you can price in minutes, steady demand, something you can test at pickup, and a size that fits in your car. Here is how common categories stack up.

Category Why it can work Check at pickup Common trap
Cordless power tools (Makita, Milwaukee, Ryobi, DeWalt) Model numbers make pricing quick; kits get split and sold as parts Run the tool under load; charge each battery; read the battery labels Tired batteries; tools sold without their origin being clear
Games consoles and controllers Known models and steady demand Power it on, load a game, test every controller button and stick Stick drift, overheating, missing cables
Phones and tablets Easy to price by model and storage Check it is erased and not locked to the owner; check battery health Activation Lock, blocked phones, hidden screen repairs
Bikes Brand and model sell the bike Frame cracks, gears, brakes, wheel true Stolen bikes; costly parts on high-end models
Furniture from known makers or solid timber Bulky pieces sell cheaper to people who cannot move them Wobble, veneer damage, smells, pests in upholstery Trailer hire and the time to move it
Cars Biggest sums A proper inspection and history checks Biggest losses; licensing rules for regular sellers (see below)

Start with one or two categories you already understand. You will price faster, spot faults sooner and know who your buyers are.

How do you work out what to pay?

Work backwards from the sale, not forwards from the asking price.

  1. Find what it actually sells for. Asking prices on Marketplace are a ceiling, not a sale price. For sold prices, use eBay’s sold or completed listings, and for cars a price guide. Our guide to checking a fair price walks through this step by step.
  2. Pick a realistic resale price. Use the lower middle of recent sales for the same model in the same condition, not the best one you found.
  3. Subtract every cost. The full list is in the next section.
  4. Subtract a margin for your time and risk. If the leftover would not be worth the effort on a bad day, the price is too high.
  5. Write down your walk-away price before you message. It stops you paying more in the excitement of a pickup.

Worked example: a cordless drill kit

Say you find a Makita 18V drill kit with two batteries and a charger, listed at A$120. Recent sold prices for the same kit run from about A$170 to A$220, so you plan to resell at A$180.

Line Amount
Resale price you expect A$180
Buy price minus A$120
Fuel: 60 km round trip at 8 L per 100 km and A$2 a litre minus about A$10
Selling fee (local sale, buyer pays you in person) A$0
Left before your time about A$50
Your time: pickup, testing, photos, messages, the sale meet-up about 2 hours

That is roughly A$25 an hour before tax, and only if it sells at A$180 and both batteries hold a charge. If one battery is weak, a replacement can cost more than the whole margin. If the buyer haggles you to A$160, you are down to about A$15 an hour. None of that is a reason not to do it; it is a reason to do the sums first.

Worked example: a dining table that barely pays

A solid timber table is listed at A$150 and similar tables have sold locally for around A$300. It looks like A$150 in hand. Now count: trailer hire (say A$40), fuel with a trailer on, a friend to help lift, sanding and oil (say A$30), a weekend of your time, and a buyer who wants it delivered. If it sits for three weeks and you drop it to A$250, you have spent a weekend to make very little. Bulky items can be good flips, but only when the transport is already sorted.

What costs should you count?

A flip priced on buy price and sale price alone is a guess. Count all of these.

Cost What to include Easy to forget because
Buy price What you actually paid after haggling It is the only number people remember
Travel Fuel both ways, tolls, parking, a second trip for a no-show Each trip feels small
Time Messages, travel, testing, cleaning, photos, relisting You are not paying yourself
Repairs and parts Batteries, tyres, screens, cleaning products Faults show up after you get home
Transport Trailer or van hire, tie-downs, a helper Bulky items look cheap until you move them
Storage The garage or spare room it sits in It feels free until stock piles up
Selling fees Fees on the platform you resell through (for example eBay, or shipped sales where Facebook offers checkout) A local, pay-in-person sale has none, so it is easy to forget the others
Checks A PPSR search in Australia, a vehicle history report, a phone block check They are small but they add up
Slow sellers Price cuts, and stock that does not sell at all You price for the best case
Tools and apps Any alert app or subscription you pay for, Zoopy included (see pricing) It is a monthly cost, not a per-item one
Tax Income tax, and in some places GST or VAT once you are big enough It arrives months later

Keep a simple ledger from day one

For each item, note the date, what you paid, every cost, what you sold it for and how long it took. After ten flips you will know which categories are worth your time, and you will have the records a tax office expects.

Do you pay tax on Marketplace flipping?

Check your local tax rules. Selling your own unwanted things now and then is usually treated differently from buying items to resell, and each country draws the line in its own way. This is general information, not tax advice.

Country What the tax office says Where to check
Australia “Generally, a business involves a set of continuous and repeated activities you do for the purpose of making a profit.” A hobby you do not seek to profit from is not a business. ATO: Are you in business?
United States Profits from selling goods, including personal items, are taxable income that must be reported. A loss on a personal item cannot be deducted. IRS: hobby or business and Form 1099-K FAQs
United Kingdom You are probably trading if you sell goods you bought intending to sell for a profit. Tell HMRC if your trading income is over the £1,000 trading allowance in a tax year. HMRC: income from online platforms
Canada Residents must report income from all sources, including peer-to-peer sales, and should keep records of sales and expenses. CRA: peer-to-peer sellers

Elsewhere, search your tax office’s website for “hobby or business” and “selling online”. If you are unsure, a registered tax agent can tell you where you stand.

Flipping cars has its own rules

In many places, regularly buying and selling cars needs a dealer licence. In Victoria, Consumer Affairs Victoria says that “generally, if you deal in four or more cars per year, you need a licence”. In Queensland, you need a motor dealer licence if you trade in used vehicles at all as part of a business. Other states, provinces and countries set their own rules, so check yours before you buy the next one.

How do you buy and sell safely?

Facebook’s own advice for meeting in person is sound: meet in a public, well-lit place, keep your home address private, tell someone you trust where you are going, keep your phone charged and avoid carrying large amounts of cash. If a meeting feels wrong, cancel it.

When you are buying:

  • See it before you pay. Scamwatch’s advice is not to pay for big-ticket items before inspecting them in person. Be wary of any seller who wants a deposit to “hold” something you have not seen.
  • Check the payee. If you pay by PayID or bank transfer, make sure the name matches the person you think you are paying.
  • Check it is not locked or owed on. For an iPhone or iPad, Apple says not to take a device that shows “iPhone Locked to Owner” or is still linked to the previous owner’s account. In Australia, AMTA’s free IMEI checker shows whether a phone has been reported lost or stolen, and a PPSR search (A$2 online at the time of writing) shows whether high-value goods such as cars, boats, caravans and trailers have money owing. In the US, CTIA’s Stolen Phone Checker does a similar job for phones.
  • Keep the chat on Facebook until the deal is done, so there is a record.

When you are selling:

  • Meet at a public place rather than your home where you can.
  • Do not hand over the item until the money is in your account. A screenshot or an email saying a payment is “pending” is not money.
  • Be wary of a buyer who wants to pay more than the price and have you refund the difference. Scamwatch lists it as a warning sign.

For the full list of tricks aimed at buyers, see Facebook Marketplace scams.

How do you watch for stock without scrolling all day?

Good stock does not wait for you to finish work. A few habits help more than endless scrolling:

  • Search for specific models, not whole categories. “Makita DHP484” finds fewer, better results than “drill”.
  • Sort by Date listed so the newest listings come first. See our search tips for the rest of the settings.
  • Check at set times instead of refreshing all day. In Zoopy’s measurements, Facebook keeps a new listing out of public search for about six to seven minutes after posting, for everyone, so refreshing every few seconds does not get you there sooner.
  • Use alerts for your best searches. Facebook can notify you about saved searches. How to set that up, and what to check if notifications stop, is in our Marketplace alerts guide.

Speed matters less than you think if you have done the sums. A flipper who knows their walk-away price and can collect today can do better than one who merely saw the listing sooner.

Questions people ask

Is flipping on Facebook Marketplace worth it?

It can be, but many flips earn less than they look once fuel, time, repairs and fees come out, and some lose money. Start small, with things you know, and do the sums before you message the seller.

What is a good first thing to flip?

Something you already understand, that is easy to price from a model number, fits in your car and is quick to test at pickup. Branded cordless tools and games consoles are common starting points.

Do I pay tax on Marketplace flipping?

It depends on your country and on whether you are clearing out your own things or buying to resell. Buying to resell again and again can count as a business. Check your tax office's guidance, such as the ATO's 'Are you in business?' page.
